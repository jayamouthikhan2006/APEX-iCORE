Pit rotation works as in section 04: the air moves, not the baskets. Each hour every role (hottest, middle, coolest, spare) shifts one pit along. That takes 6 levers and 1 minute, instead of 3 hot basket lifts. From a cold start the scrap warms from 28 to 42 kWh/t over a 16-hour day; the year average is 41.1 kWh/t into the scrap.

The physics behind 52 kWh per tonne

Counterflow, matched
  • Castings 0.17 kW/K against air 0.15 kW/K.
  • So the air leaves within about 100 K of 500°C.
Why downflow
  • Bed friction 0.1–0.2 Pa against hot-air buoyancy 0.3–2 Pa.
  • Gravity steers the air, so it must flow down through the scrap.
Settling (Stokes)
  • 100 µm sand falls at 0.44 m/s.
  • The box air moves at 0.3 m/s, so the sand drops out.
Fans and pressure
  • ≈ 1,000 Pa budget, 700 of it the filter.
  • Exhaust fan ≈ 0.29 kW; all running power 6,294 kWh a year.

01 · The idea

Casting heat goes back into the furnace

Every foundry cools hot castings in open air and melts cold scrap with electricity. Heat Boomerang connects the two.

SourceHot castings500°C after shakeout
→
CarrierAirpulled through by a fan
→
AbsorberScrappreheated in buckets
→
UserFurnacemelts it with less electricity

There is no special heat-absorbing material. Air picks up heat from the castings, and the scrap that has to be melted anyway soaks it up. The heat changes hands once, so very little is lost.

Why the scrap is the absorber

You could store the heat in something else first. Each extra step loses heat and adds cost, and the scrap has to be melted anyway.

AbsorberHow the heat reaches the furnaceTransfersVerdict
Scrap (steel)Air heats the scrap, and the scrap goes straight into the furnace1Chosen. No extra material, no second transfer
Firebrick or stonesAir heats brick, then more air takes heat from the brick to the scrap2Backup only, when no basket is ready
Water or steamBoils at 100°C, so it needs pressure piping2+Rejected. Unsafe near molten metal
Thermal oilOil loop to a heat exchanger, then to the scrap2+Rejected. Fire risk at 400°C
Phase-change saltSalt tank with a heat exchanger in and out2+Rejected. Costly, and the scrap does the same job for free
Electricity (TEG or ORC)Heat → electricity → furnace2+Rejected. Only 5–10% comes back

Steel stores about 0.5 kJ per kg per °C. One tonne heated from 35°C to 326°C (the locked design's mean) holds about 41 kWh, which the furnace then does not have to supply.

    04 · Scrap station (locked design, superseded by Rev S)

    Five pits, one hot lift an hour

    Superseded by Rev S (section S1). Rev S keeps this station's principles (downflow, sand seals, light baskets, moving the hot end instead of the baskets) with 4 pits, 3 in series, and 12 manual dampers.

    No pipes are fixed to the buckets. Five insulated pits carry all the ductwork. A light basket of scrap drops into a pit, a hood seals on top, and hot air flows down through it. Dampers move the hot end around the five pits every hour, so only one basket is lifted: the hot one going to the furnace.

    One station

    Fixed parts (the pit)
    • Steel box about 1.5 × 1.5 × 2.3 m, lined with 300 mm ceramic-fibre blanket, faced with thin polished stainless sheet.
    • Two sand troughs round the top: the hood skirt dips into the outer one, the basket's knife edge into the inner one.
    • Plenum under the basket with one side port to the link and exhaust dampers.
    • Hood on a pneumatic lift with a stainless bellows, so the ductwork never moves.
    • A thermocouple in the hood and one in the plenum, plus one pressure tap.
    Moving part (the basket)
    • Solid 1.5–2 mm sides on a light angle frame, about 200 kg for 1 t of scrap. No lining, because anything on the basket heats and cools every trip.
    • Top flange with a knife edge in the inner sand trough, so air cannot slip down outside the basket.
    • Two perforated drop-bottom doors, 20% open, latched. They are both the air outlet and the way the scrap leaves.
    • Loading: heavy returns on top (hottest), steel scrap in the middle, small pieces on a mesh insert at the bottom. Keep oily turnings out.

    The hourly change-over

    Written for the change from hour 1 to hour 2 (B1 in S1 leaves). The PLC does steps 1–3, and one crane operator does the rest.

    1. Open H2, close H1 and L1. Hot air now enters S2 first. S1 is cut out of the chain with its scrap at about 326°C.
    2. Close X4, open L4 and X5. The spare S5, refilled with cold scrap, joins as the last station.
    3. Open the small vent on S1, then lift its hood 200 mm. S1 is now at shop pressure, so no hot air puffs out.
    4. Lift the basket out and carry it to the feed hopper, about 2 minutes. Open the drop-bottom doors over the hopper and close the hopper lid.
    5. Close the empty S1 hood so the pit stays warm. The basket goes to the scrap bay, is loaded (heavy on top), and returns to S1 within about 35 minutes. S1 waits as the spare.
    6. The vibratory feeder meters the hot scrap into the furnace through the melt.

    07 · Physics

    How the model works

    Every equation behind the numbers, with a worked example.

    The design numbers come from a one-dimensional transient model. The tunnel is cut into short slices (70 slices for 20 m). Every 2 seconds the model balances heat for the castings, the wall and the air in each slice, then passes the air to the next slice and the scrap buckets. Every energy balance closes within 0.03 kWh per tonne.

    A · The base model, slice by slice

    Castings radiate to the wall

    q_rad = σ (T_c⁴ − T_w⁴) / R R = (1−ε_c)/(ε_c A_c) + 1/(A_c F_cw) + (1−ε_w)/(ε_w A_w) σ = 5.67×10⁻⁸ W/m²K⁴, ε = 0.8

    Temperatures in kelvin. Radiation grows with the fourth power of temperature, so it dominates at the hot end.

    Castings and wall heat the air

    q_conv = h_c A_c (T_c − T_air) q_w = h_w A_w · (fin multiplier) · (T_w − T_air)

    Energy balances

    air: ṁ c_p (T_out − T_in) = q_conv + q_w casting: m c_p dT_c/dt = −(q_rad + q_conv) wall: C_w dT_w/dt = q_rad − q_w − q_loss, q_loss = U A_out (T_w − 35)

    The wall is the middleman. It takes the castings' radiation and hands it to the air. That is why fins and cross-fans, which help the wall pass heat on to the air, raise the output.

    B · What Rev C added

    Casting carrier

    entry: h_mix = (m_c h(500) + m_car h(T_return)) / (m_c + m_car) exit loss = m_car [h(T_exit) − h(T_return)]

    Apron: 20 kg per set, returns at 60°C, 80% of casting surface exposed. Hooks: 2 kg, 50°C, fully exposed. Instant contact equilibrium at entry is the pessimistic bound for an apron.

    View factor to the wall

    q_rad = σ(T_c⁴ − T_w⁴) / [ (1−ε_c)/(ε_c A_c) + 1/(A_c F_cw) + (1−ε_w)/(ε_w A_w) ] F_cw = 0.90 hooks, 0.85 apron

    Fins from geometry

    m = √(2h / (k t)) η_f = tanh(mH)/(mH) multiplier = (1 − t/p) + η_f · 2H/p (k = 45 W/m·K)

    Recomputed in every segment every step from the local h. Fin steel is added to the wall heat capacity.

    Recirculation

    wall: fully turbulent plate Nu = 0.037 Re^0.8 Pr^⅓ (L = 1 m, u = u_rec) casting: Churchill–Bernstein at v_through + u_rec fan power per zone = Q Δp / η, Q = u·(4 m × 1 m), Δp = 4 · ρu²/2, η = 0.45

    Insulation that changes with temperature

    k(T) = 0.035 + 0.00015 · T_mean W/m·K U = 1 / (t/k + 1/10)

    Packed-bed buckets

    6 layers per bucket, air bottom → top d_p = 6(1−ε)/a_v Nu_p = 2 + 1.1 Re_p^0.6 Pr^⅓ (Wakao–Kaguei) per layer: T_out = T_s + (T_in − T_s) e^(−hA/ṁc_p)

    1 t of 6 mm scrap: 42.5 m² of surface, void fraction 0.95, d_p ≈ 18 mm, h ≈ 30–40 W/m²K. Ergun pressure drop is under 1 Pa; each bucket's grid and ports are taken as 150 Pa.

    Electricity saved at the furnace

    saved = Q_scrap / η_induction, η = 0.75 (coil and converter losses) net = saved − fan electricity

    Each kWh of heat already in the scrap is a kWh the coil does not have to deliver at about 75% efficiency. The shorter melt time saves more on top; it is not counted.

    With these effects switched off, the engine reproduces the earlier Rev A result exactly (41.6 kWh/t to air, 24.9 to scrap).

    C · System-level physics used across this handbook

    How much heat exists

    Q = m c_p ΔT castings 500 → 35°C: 1,000 kg × ~0.55 kJ/kg·K × 465 K ÷ 3,600 ≈ 71 kWh per tonne

    This is the ceiling for the tunnel. Rev C releases 60.3 kWh/t of it in the tunnel. No fin or fan can create more.

    How much the scrap can take

    Q_scrap = 1,000 kg × c_p × (T_charge − 35) ÷ 3,600 326°C mean → about 41 kWh/t (locked station: 41.4 in scrap, 39.7 into the melt) 400°C cap → about 51 kWh/t

    Above about 400°C the scrap oxidises and metal is lost, so the scrap caps what can be returned.

    Buckets in series, hottest first

    per layer: T_out = T_s + (T_in − T_s) · e^(−hA / ṁc_p)

    Air leaves each layer close to that layer's scrap temperature. Passing the air last through the coldest bucket brings the exhaust down toward 35°C (Rev C: about 45°C). It can never go below the coldest scrap or the shop air.

    Electricity saved

    saved = Q_melt / η_induction = 39.69 / 0.75 = 52.92 kWh/t net = saved − fans − feeder = 52.92 − 1.30 = 51.62 kWh/t

    Fan power

    P = V̇ · Δp / η e.g. 0.16 m³/s × 2,200 Pa / 0.55 ≈ 0.64 kW

    Every extra grid, mesh or bend adds pressure drop Δp and costs fan power.

    Dew point (wet-scrap and loop limit)

    e = w·p / (0.622 + w) γ = ln(e / 6.112 hPa) T_dew = 243.04 · γ / (17.625 − γ)

    Steam from sand and scrap raises w. If T_dew comes within 3°C of the coldest scrap, water can condense on it, so the loop purges.

    Why pressurising a bucket is pointless

    air in a bucket ≈ 2.6 m³ × 0.55 kg/m³ ≈ 1.5 kg heat it carries ≈ 1.5 × 1.05 × (370 − 35) ÷ 3,600 ≈ 0.15 kWh

    The heat sits in the tonne of scrap, not in the air around it.

    D · Scrap station physics

    Why the air must flow downward

    Ergun Δp/H = 150 μ(1−ε)²u / (ε³d²) + 1.75 ρ(1−ε)u² / (ε³d) ε = 0.951, d = 18 mm, H = 1.8 m buoyancy Δp_b = g H (ρ̄_column − ρ_inlet) porous Ra = g β ΔT K H / (ν α), K = d²ε³ / (150(1−ε)²) = 7.69×10⁻⁴ m², critical 4π² ≈ 39.5

    The scrap bed is 95% empty space, so buoyancy is 3–14 times stronger than bed friction. Blown upward, hot light air chimneys up a few channels (Ra = 1.4×10⁶, about 36,000 times critical) and leaves most of the scrap cold. Blown downward, the air cools as it goes, the heaviest air is at the bottom, and any path that runs hot gets pushed back. The bed evens itself out. The distributor plate's 35 Pa, about 18 times the largest buoyancy head, sets an even start.

    Automatic exhaust sequence

    1. Fresh inlet. D-701 and D-703 stack outlet open. Used at start-up and whenever the air needs cleaning.
    2. Stack loop. Once the stack is warm and the air is clean, D-701 and D-703 close and D-702 opens. The warm exhaust goes back into the harvester.

    14 · Before building

    What to verify at a real foundry

    • Shakeout temperature of every part family. The model assumes 500°C for every set.
    • Casting surface area. The model assumes 0.01 m² per kg and emissivity 0.8.
    • Induction furnace efficiency. The model assumes 0.75. Measure it on EM-001 with preheated and cold heats of the same grade.
    • Scrap oil, paint and moisture. These set how long the stack loop can run and how often it purges.
    • Recirculation fans rated for about 400°C. The heat-transfer figures assume their jets make the wall flow turbulent.
    • Even air through each bucket. A good bottom grid is essential.
    • Castings that cannot hang (large frames) need a bypass lane.
    • Scrap bulk density. The model's scrap is 1 t of 6 mm pieces at 95% voids in a 1.2 × 1.2 × 1.8 m bed. Real charge is denser. The downflow and seal rules still hold, but the numbers shift.
    • Fibre lining cycling heat is the biggest uncertainty on the scrap side. Thermocouples in a trial pit will settle it.
    • Sand seals must be kept dry and topped up. Watch the hood pressure taps.
    • Oily turnings release fumes in B1 that condense on colder scrap downstream. Keep them out or de-oil them first.
    • Basket heat is taken as fully lost each trip, which is slightly pessimistic.
    • Capital costs are planning estimates. Replace them with quotes.
